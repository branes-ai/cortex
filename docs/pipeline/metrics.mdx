Every stage page reports its health in **native units**. This page is the shared
primer for the metrics they use, so the stage pages can stay focused. There are
two distinct questions:

1. **Is the estimate accurate?** — how close is it to truth. (ATE, RPE)
2. **Is the estimate's claimed uncertainty honest?** — does the covariance match
   the real error. (NEES, NIS, innovation whiteness)

The second is the one most people skip, and it is the one that kills missions.

## Accuracy — ATE and RPE

- **ATE (Absolute Trajectory Error)** — RMS position error between the estimate
  and ground truth after a rigid (Horn) alignment. One number for "how far off,
  globally." Sensitive to drift accumulating over the whole run.
- **RPE (Relative Pose Error)** — error over short fixed-length sub-trajectories.
  Measures **drift rate** (e.g. % of distance), which for long missions matters
  more than a single global number.

Accuracy tells you nothing about whether the filter *knows* it's wrong.

## Consistency — the calibration of the uncertainty

A Kalman-type filter produces an estimate **and** a statement of its own
uncertainty, the covariance `P`. When VIO says "the camera is here," it means
"I'm 68% sure it's within this ellipsoid." A filter is **consistent** when that
self-report matches reality: it says ±10 cm and is actually wrong by ~10 cm — not
1 m (**over-confident**, lying that it's sure) and not 1 mm (**under-confident**,
wasting information).

<Aside type="tip" title="The weather-forecaster analogy">
  A forecaster who says "70% chance of rain" is *well-calibrated* if, across all
  the days they said 70%, it actually rained about 70% of the time. NEES and NIS
  are that calibration check for an estimator — they don't ask "is the estimate
  close?", they ask "is the estimate's *claimed uncertainty* honest?"
</Aside>

### The one idea: "how many sigmas off?"

For a scalar estimate `x̂` with standard deviation `σ` and truth `x`, the error
`e = x̂ − x` measured in units of σ is `z = e/σ`, and `z² = e²/σ²` has expected
value **1** if the filter is honest. Average it over many estimates: ≈ 1 is
honest, ≫ 1 means real errors exceed what σ claims (over-confident), ≪ 1 means
under-confident.

In many dimensions the state is correlated, so `1/σ²` becomes the inverse
covariance and `e²/σ²` becomes the **squared Mahalanobis distance**:

```text
  d² = eᵀ P⁻¹ e
```

If the filter is consistent, `d² ~ χ²(n)` with `n` the dimension, and the key fact
is `E[χ²(n)] = n`. So divide by the dimension and **a consistent filter sits at
1.0**. Everything below is "measure `d²/n` and check it's near 1."

### NEES vs NIS — same idea, two places

Both are the dof-normalized Mahalanobis distance above; they differ only in *what
error* and *what covariance* you plug in:

- **NEES** — **N**ormalized **E**stimation **E**rror **S**quared: `eᵀ P⁻¹ e` on
  the **state estimate's** error vs the **state** covariance `P`.
- **NIS** — **N**ormalized **I**nnovation **S**quared: the same form on the
  **innovation** (measurement − prediction) vs the **innovation** covariance `S`.

| | NEES | NIS |
|---|---|---|
| error | `x̂ − x_true` (state error) | `z − ẑ` (innovation: measured − predicted) |
| covariance | `P` (state covariance) | `S = HPHᵀ + R` (innovation covariance) |
| dof | state dim (15 in the cortex core) | measurement dim |
| needs truth? | **yes** → offline (EuRoC) | **no** → online, always |

- **NEES** tests the *whole state's* calibration, including directions
  measurements don't directly observe. Per-state-block NEES (slice out attitude,
  or velocity) localizes **which** state is over/under-confident.
- **NIS** tests the filter against its *own incoming measurements* — no ground
  truth — so it runs live on the robot and powers the χ² outlier gate.

**NIS consistent while NEES is inconsistent** is itself a clue: the measurement
model is fine, but a hidden state (a bias, a scale, an unmodeled calibration) is
drifting.

### From one number to a verdict

A single `d²` is very noisy (χ²(n) has standard deviation `√(2n)`, comparable to
its mean). The test averages over a run: the sum of `N` samples is `χ²(D)` with
`D = Σ dofᵢ`, which for large `D` is ≈ `Normal(D, 2D)`. The dof-normalized average
`S/D` lands near **1.0** with a tight confidence band, turning a noisy stream into
a verdict: **consistent**, **over-confident** (`> 1`, the dangerous one), or
**under-confident** (`< 1`). The two-sided $(1-\alpha)$ acceptance band is
$1 \pm z\sqrt{2/D}$ with $z = \Phi^{-1}(1-\alpha/2)$ ($z = 1.96$ at 95%): the
classic Bar-Shalom NEES/NIS consistency test. The band tightens as the run grows.

**The per-update χ² gate is a different use of the same statistic.** Before
applying a measurement, the filter checks its NIS against a χ² threshold and
rejects a wild outlier (a mistracked feature). That is a single-sample *gate*; the
consistency test is a *run average*. Both compute the same `νᵀ S⁻¹ ν`, and #264
logs the gate's value so the consistency test can run on it. The shipped gate
accepts γ ≤ 5 per dof, which is loose at low dof
([#481](https://github.com/branes-ai/cortex/issues/481)).

### Innovation whiteness

NIS tests the innovation's *magnitude*. **Whiteness** tests its *structure*: a
healthy innovation sequence is zero-mean and temporally uncorrelated (white). A
**biased mean** points at a systematic error (extrinsics, triangulation,
time-sync); **temporal correlation** points at unmodeled dynamics or a
linearization/observability inconsistency — failure modes a magnitude test cannot
see.

## How cortex computes them

The quadratic form `eᵀ P⁻¹ e` is `normalized_squared(e, P)` in
`eval/consistency.hpp` (a Cholesky solve, never an explicit inverse). NIS is
accumulated on every camera update (`nis_consistency()`); innovation whiteness is
the directional/temporal discriminator (`innovation_whiteness()`). The headers are
under `sdk/include/branes/sdk/`.

| Piece | Header | Role |
|---|---|---|
| `normalized_squared` / `nis` / `nees` | `eval/consistency.hpp` | the `eᵀ P⁻¹ e` form; a Cholesky solve that throws on a non-positive-definite covariance |
| `ConsistencyAccumulator` / `ConsistencyReport` | `eval/consistency.hpp` | `add(d², dof)` per sample; `report()` gives the normalized average and the band verdict |
| per-update NIS | `msckf/camera_updater.hpp` → `MsckfBackend::nis_consistency()` | the gate's `γ = νᵀ S⁻¹ ν`, recorded on every camera update |
| `nav_error` / `gauge_align` / `align_truth` / `core_covariance` | `eval/nav_consistency.hpp` | the 15-vector state error against ground truth, in the filter's convention and frame |
| EuRoC report | `tests/sdk/vio_euroc.cpp` | warns with the run's NIS and NEES verdicts |

### Two VIO-specific subtleties

1. **The error lives in the filter's tangent space and ordering.** The state error
   is `[δθ δp δv δbg δba]`, with the attitude error a rotation on the manifold,
   `δθ = Log(R̂⁻¹ R_true)`, not a quaternion difference. `nav_error` builds exactly
   this, so it matches the layout of `P`.
2. **The gauge is yaw-only, not SE(3).** A VIO solution is fixed only up to four
   unobservable degrees of freedom: global position and yaw about gravity. Roll and
   pitch are observable, because the accelerometer feels gravity. The estimate and
   the truth therefore live in different world frames, and only that 4-DoF gauge may
   be removed before differencing. `gauge_align` builds a yaw-only rotation plus a
   translation (Zhang & Scaramuzza's "posyaw" alignment). A full 6-DoF SE(3)
   alignment would rotate away the observable roll/pitch error and report a
   flatteringly small NEES, a bug caught in review.

## Reading the verdict

| Reading | Meaning | First suspects |
|---|---|---|
| normalized ≈ 1, in band | **consistent**: the covariance honestly bounds the error | (healthy) |
| normalized above the band (over-confident) | real errors exceed the claimed uncertainty | process noise `Q` too small; a wrong or sign-flipped Jacobian; an unmodeled bias making residuals systematically biased |
| normalized below the band (under-confident) | the claimed uncertainty exceeds the real errors | measurement noise `R` too large; over-inflated `Q`; the filter ignoring usable information |
| **NIS consistent, NEES ≫ 1** | innovations fine, *state* drifting unmodeled | a systematic scale or bias error the covariance doesn't represent (a prime #212 signature) |
| per-block NEES spikes in one state | that state is the culprit | e.g. attitude-block NEES climbing first ⇒ gyro bias or the propagation Jacobian |

A divergence or a drift now has a *direction*: not "it's wrong, edit something and
re-measure ATE," but "the filter is over-confident, in the attitude block first,
from the second propagation," which points at a component and a term (see the
[diagnostic methodology](diagnostic-methodology.mdx)).

## Caveats

- **It is a large-sample test.** The Gaussian band assumes hundreds to thousands of
  updates; on a handful of samples it is meaningless. At the nominal α a perfectly
  consistent filter is still flagged α of the time by chance, so treat a marginal
  verdict as a prompt to look, not a proof.
- **Samples within one run are correlated.** The band assumes independent χ² terms;
  consecutive frames of one run are not, so the sum's variance can exceed 2D and the band
  is too narrow. A single-run verdict is approximate; independent Monte Carlo runs give the
  exact test.
- **The covariance and the error must be the same object:** the same tangent space,
  state ordering and units. A NEES against a mismatched `P` is nonsense, which is
  why `nav_error` and `core_covariance` are tied to `msckf::State`'s documented
  layout.
- **A non-positive-definite covariance is itself a failure.** `normalized_squared`
  throws on it, and the EuRoC report counts and surfaces any frames it had to skip.
