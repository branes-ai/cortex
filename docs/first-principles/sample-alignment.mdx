A sensor-fusion pipeline combines streams that sample the world at different times: an IMU at
200 Hz, a camera at 20 Hz, perhaps a GNSS receiver at 10 Hz. **Every sample stands for a time.**
A computation that uses a sample at any other time adds a skew between the streams. That skew is
an unmodelled time offset, and its error grows with how fast the signal changes.

cortex's IMU propagation did exactly this from its first version until
<Issue id={616} />. Both VIO backends held one IMU sample constant over each 5 ms interval, and
they held different samples. This page explains the failure mode, how it stayed hidden, how it
was caught, and what to do in any pipeline that integrates one sensor between the samples of
another.

## What a sample stands for

Before integrating a stream, write down the time each sample represents. It is not always the
timestamp.

| Sample | What it stands for | Use it as |
|---|---|---|
| An instantaneous rate (most MEMS gyros as delivered, a synthetic IMU) | the rate at its timestamp | one end of the interval it bounds; interpolate between samples |
| An increment, Δθ or Δv (many tactical IMUs) | the integral over the interval that ends at its timestamp | that interval's increment, with a coning correction; never as a rate held over the next interval |
| A filtered or decimated sample (the ADIS16448 on EuRoC's rig) | the rate a filter group delay earlier | an instantaneous rate shifted by that delay, which calibration has to find |
| A global-shutter image | the middle of the exposure, offset from the trigger stamp | a measurement at that time, through the camera–IMU time offset t_d |
| A rolling-shutter image | a different time on each row | per-row times, or a model of the readout |

When two devices are synchronized in hardware, as on EuRoC, the clock is right but the meaning of
each sample can still be off by a fraction of its interval.

## How a hold becomes a skew

An IMU interval [t₍ₖ₋₁₎, tₖ] of length Δt has a sample at each end. Propagation has to choose
an input over the interval, and each choice implies a time:

| Input over the interval | Equivalent to | Who did it in cortex |
|---|---|---|
| The end sample, held | the IMU read Δt/2 **early** | `MsckfBackend`, <Issue id={186} /> to <Issue id={616} /> |
| The start sample, held | the IMU read Δt/2 **late** | `InvariantVioBackend`, <Issue id={608} /> to <Issue id={616} /> |
| The line between the two samples | no skew to first order | both backends since <Issue id={616} />; OpenVINS |

At 200 Hz, Δt/2 is 2.5 ms. Holding the end sample integrates ∫ω dt as ω(t_k)·Δt, which is
ω(t_mid)·Δt + ω̇·Δt²/2. Summed over a window, the rotation error is the change in rate over the
window times the skew: **δθ ≈ Δω · Δt/2**. EuRoC's fast sequences roll and pitch at up to
1.3 rad/s (the 95th percentile). On a test flight at that rate the rate changes by about 1.2 rad/s
across a 0.55 s clone window, and the error reaches 3.1 mrad: 1.4 px at cam0's focal length, 28×
what the camera resolves. The camera then measures the attitude far more precisely than
propagation delivers it.

The integrator adds a second, separate error. A single Euler step rotates the specific force with
the attitude at the *start* of the interval. With the body turning at 1.3 rad/s, that rotation is
2.5 ms stale on average too, and it shows up as a first-order drift in velocity and position.
Centring the input doesn't fix it; integrating along the interval does.

## Why it hides

A half-sample skew rarely looks like itself. In cortex it appeared as four other things:

- **Ground-truth error.** EuRoC's Vicon ground truth disagrees with the gyro by 0.25–0.45 px over
  0.25 s in the Vicon rooms. The skew sits at the same size, so a consistency test against the real
  IMU can't tell the two apart (<Issue id={613} />).
- **A calibration problem.** A constant skew between IMU and camera is exactly what the
  camera–IMU time offset t_d models. An online t_d estimator (<Issue id={332} />) would have learned
  a backend-specific 2.5 ms, and the defect would have looked calibrated away.
- **Over-confidence.** NIS was 1.02–1.04 on the slow Machine Hall sequences but 1.1–1.9 on V1_03,
  V2_02 and V2_03, which turn 3.5–4× faster. The over-confidence investigation of
  <Issue id={212} /> had a dozen candidates, and a speed-dependent NIS fits several of them.
- **Nothing at all.** The error scales with angular *acceleration*. The synthetic test world only
  yaws, gently, so it never excited the error, and every unit test of S2 passed.

<Aside type="caution" title="The tell">
  An error that grows with how fast the signal *changes*, and is small on slow segments, points at
  timing before it points at noise models. Split NIS, NEES and the closure error by motion speed
  before tuning anything.
</Aside>

## How cortex was caught

| When | What happened | Issue |
|---|---|---|
| 2026-05 | The MSCKF backend was built in a day from the standard error-state EKF derivation. The one-sample Euler step is the first thing those texts derive. The driver passes each interval the sample that ends it. | <Issue id={186} /> |
| 2026-06 | The S0–S10 stage contracts state that OpenVINS integrates "with a zeroth-order hold or RK4". The S2 probe went after the covariance suspects of #212 (the diagonal Q_d, the structure of Φ) and cleared S2. The mean was never checked against a continuous truth at the real rate. | <Issue id={212} />, <Issue id={283} /> |
| 2026-06 | The same S2 probe measured the mean against ground-truth injection and recorded "integrator order O(Δt): the error halves with Δt, 59 → 30 → 15 → 7 mm". First order is the defect itself. It went into a metrics table with no bound, so nothing failed. | <Issue id={283} /> |
| 2026-10 | "A more accurate Φ makes the yaw leak worse" (#471) came to read as "a higher-order S2 doesn't help". The lesson was about the covariance, not the mean. | <Issue id={471} /> |
| 2026-11 | The R-IEKF backend got its own driver, which holds the *previous* sample. The two backends now disagreed by 5 ms. | <Issue id={608} /> |
| 2026-11 | The consistency gate was rebuilt on one smooth truth: a B-spline through the ground truth feeds the synthesized IMU, the ideal tracks and the score, so R and Q are exact. NIS was 1.00 on the slow sequences and 1.1–1.9 on the fast ones. | <Issue id={613} /> |
| 2026-11 | Probes changed one thing at a time (below). Only centring the input removed the excess. | <Issue id={616} /> |

The probes ran on the MSCKF on #613's exact truth, at 0.5 px with matched R:

| S2 variant | V2_03 4-DoF ATE | V2_03 NIS | MH_01 NIS |
|---|---|---|---|
| shipped before #616: end sample held, start attitude | 0.19–0.24 m | 1.77–1.80 | 1.03 |
| specific force rotated at the midpoint attitude | 0.18–0.21 m | 1.76–1.80 | 1.03 |
| 5 substeps on the same held sample | 0.19–0.23 m | 1.79–1.80 | — |
| **the average of the interval's two samples** | **0.013–0.018 m** | **1.00** | **1.00** |
| IMU synthesized at 1 kHz, shipped S2 | 0.03 m | 1.02 | — |

Substeps didn't help, so the integrator's order wasn't the cause. The attitude the specific force
is rotated with didn't help either. The cause was the input: a sample held over an interval it
doesn't represent.

### The test that blessed the defect

While building #613's gate, I wrote an S2 closure test: integrate the spline's own noise-free IMU
and compare with the spline. It measured S2's error, saw that the error halved when the step
halved, and called it "discretization, not a disagreement". I set its threshold just above what
S2 achieved. The test was reporting a first-order defect at 28× what the camera resolves, and I
signed it off, because I took its tolerance from the code's current residual rather than from what
the next stage has to resolve.

This was the fifth result in cortex's VIO work that came from the test setup rather than the
estimator ([Chapter 5](storyline.mdx#chapter-5-when-the-test-lies)). It produced two rules in the
[paper-to-pipeline method](../methodology/overview.mdx) (<Issue id={617} />):

- **Every option the paper offers is a recorded decision with a reason.** "Zeroth-order hold or
  RK4" named the choice, but nobody made it, and two drivers made it differently by default.
- **Every tolerance comes from what the consuming stage must resolve.** The closure bound is a
  tenth of the 0.5 px track noise over a clone window: 1.09e-4 rad, and 0.22 mm at a 2 m depth.

## What fixed it

Both backends now integrate each interval from both of its samples, with one convention stated in
`sdk/include/branes/sdk/msckf/stages/s2_propagation.hpp`:

- **The input** is the line between the two samples.
- **The mean** is RK4 on that input (`rk4_strapdown` in `propagator.hpp`). With a rate linear in
  time, the attitude has a closed form: the rate's integral plus the coning term. Velocity and
  position are then quadratures, evaluated at the start, middle and end of the interval.
- **The covariance** propagates with Φ, the exact Jacobian of that mean. The first-order Φ had
  been chosen (#471) because it agreed best with the old Euler mean. Against the RK4 mean it leaked
  7× more yaw per step, so Φ had to follow the mean. Now it preserves the unobservable directions
  to roundoff.
- **An image between two samples:** propagate to the image time holding the last sample, since the
  next one hasn't arrived. When it does, integrate the rest of the interval from the rate
  interpolated at the image time. The residual skew lives only on that partial interval, and is
  zero on EuRoC, where image and IMU stamps coincide.

The closure test (`tests/tools/gt_spline.cpp`) separates the two errors, on a flight that rolls
and pitches at about 1.3 rad/s, against the camera-derived bound:

| Scheme | Rotation ÷ bound (200 Hz) | Position ÷ bound | Order |
|---|---|---|---|
| End sample held, one Euler step (MSCKF before #616) | 28.6 | 41 | first |
| Start sample held, one Euler step (R-IEKF before #616) | 28.0 | 6.9 | first |
| The two samples' average, one Euler step | 0.28 | 24 | first, in position |
| The two samples' average, RK4 | 0.28 | 0.16 | second |
| **The line between the samples, RK4 (shipped)** | **0.27** | **0.10** | **second** |

Centring the input fixes the rotation. Integrating along the interval fixes the position. The
order is second, not fourth: RK4 integrates the line exactly enough, but the line follows the true
rate only to second order between samples, and that is the error that remains, a quarter of the
bound.

## What it changed

On the consistency gate's spline truth, where R and Q are exact (recorded at f1551cc,
[EuRoC Baseline](../benchmarks/euroc-baseline.mdx#results-with-one-truth-and-s2-integrating-both-samples-616)):

| | MSCKF before → after | R-IEKF before → after |
|---|---|---|
| NIS on V1_03, V2_02, V2_03 (0.5 px) | 1.23–1.80 → **1.00** | 1.30–1.87 → **1.00** |
| V2_03 window ATE ÷ OpenVINS mono (0.5 px) | 0.9× → **0.1×** | 1.4× → **0.1×** |
| Matched R beats the default R (condition 2) | fail → **pass** | fail at 1 px → **pass** |
| S2's share of the spurious yaw information, real EuRoC MH_05 / V2_03 (observability ledger, #480) | 0.9% / 5.3% → **0.0%** | — (no leak by construction) |

On the real EuRoC IMU and the real KLT tracks the gain is much smaller: the median
window ATE goes from 2.83× to 2.57× OpenVINS mono for the MSCKF and from 2.62× to
2.44× for the R-IEKF, and the R-IEKF's V1_03 halves. **The real-data NIS doesn't
move:** it stays at 6.7–15.5 on the same fast sequences where the spline truth went
to 1.00. So the half-sample skew was real and is gone, but it isn't what makes the
filters over-confident on real data. That remains open, and the candidates are the
ones this page warns about: the ADIS16448's own filter delay, the camera–IMU time
offset, and the front end's outliers. The last item of the checklist below is not
hypothetical.

## A checklist for any fusion pipeline

1. **Write down what time each sample stands for**: instantaneous, an increment over the past
   interval, delayed by a filter, the middle of an exposure. Put it in the stage contract.
2. **State one integration convention, and use it everywhere.** Say what input the integrator sees
   over an interval, and what happens to an event between two samples. Two implementations of the
   same stage must not choose differently by default.
3. **Interpolate between samples; never hold one over an interval it doesn't represent.** An
   increment sample is already its interval's integral: use it as one.
4. **Integrate along the interval.** Rotate the specific force with the attitude at each point of
   the interval, not the attitude at its start.
5. **Re-derive the covariance from the new mean.** A transition matrix chosen because it agreed
   with the old integrator disagrees with the new one, and the disagreement is an information
   leak. Take Φ as the exact Jacobian of the mean you ship, and check it against finite differences.
6. **Test against a continuous truth at the real rate and the real dynamics.** Integrate a smooth
   trajectory's own noise-free samples and close on the trajectory. Take the bound from what the
   next measurement resolves, check the convergence order, and pin the alternative you rejected as
   failing.
7. **Excite the error.** It scales with angular acceleration times the skew, so test on the
   fastest segments, not only on a gentle synthetic world.
8. **Split the consistency metrics by motion speed.** An NIS that rises with speed points at
   timing before it points at noise.
9. **Sweep the skew.** Shift one stream by fractions of its interval and watch NIS. The minimum is
   the effective offset the pipeline is running with.
10. **Fix the convention before estimating t_d.** An online time-offset state absorbs any constant
   skew, including a software one, and then the calibration carries the bug.
11. **Don't assume synthetic gains carry over.** A real IMU's internal filtering adds its own
    delay, and the dataset's calibration has its own time shift; measure the change on real data.
